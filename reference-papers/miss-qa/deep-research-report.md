# Executive Summary

Zhao *et al.* (ACL 2025) present **MISS-QA**, a new benchmark for diagram-based QA in scientific literature. The dataset contains **~1.5K expert-annotated questions** (across ~465 papers) where each question ties to a schematic overview figure from a research paper【31†L54-L63】【4†L118-L123】. Models see the diagram (with a highlighted element), its caption, and relevant text, and must answer an *information-seeking* question. About **22.5% of questions are unanswerable** (the model should answer “I do not know”)【4†L125-L132】. The authors evaluate **18 multimodal LLMs** (OpenAI’s GPT-4o/4.1 and derivatives, Google Gemini-2.5, Qwen2-VL family, InternVL, Phi-Vision, Mistral-Vision, Pixtral, etc.) using a chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt that instructs step-by-step reasoning【7†L517-L525】. Accuracy (0/0.5/1) is measured by an automated LLM judge (GPT-4 in initial reports)【7†L490-L498】. 

Key findings: **All models lag far behind humans**. Two PhD experts scored ~89% accuracy on a sample of questions, whereas the best open model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) only reached ~61.6%【7†L545-L552】【13†L584-L591】. Proprietary systems (GPT-4.1, o4-mini) are better but still well below expert level. Notably, models often **hallucinate** answers on unanswerable questions, while humans correctly abstain【13†L604-L612】. Detailed error analysis reveals five major failure modes: (1) *Failure to interpret the diagram* (model cannot identify the highlighted element or structure), (2) *Failure to retrieve context* (even with text, the model misses the relevant section), (3) *Reasoning errors* (it finds relevant info but draws wrong conclusions), (4) *Overconfidence on unanswerable questions* (model answers when it shouldn’t), and (5) *Over-reliance on visuals* (ignoring text context and relying on visual guess)【16†L755-L764】【16†L771-L779】. For example, one model identified the wrong component in a DNN diagram and omitted the key point about “dynamic incremental updating”【16†L733-L742】. 

**Contribution & Context:** MISS-QA is the first benchmark focused on *schematic diagram understanding* in scientific papers【31†L54-L63】【23†L133-L141】. It complements recent work on visual programming tasks (e.g. Parsons problems and flowcharts) by adding context-rich, text+image QA. The study uses a CoT prompt adapted from previous multimodal QA work【7†L517-L525】. It is rigorous: data and code are released publicly (GitHub and HuggingFace) and model evaluation is systematic. The authors’ analysis pinpoints why current multimodal models fail and suggests directions to improve their fidelity. 

**Strengths:** The paper provides a **carefully curated dataset** of real research diagrams with expert answers, reflecting realistic information-seeking use cases. It evaluates a broad set of models and strategies, including proprietary and open-source LLMs, giving a clear state-of-the-art snapshot. The use of a CoT prompt and an LLM-based judge for scoring are methodologically sound. Their thorough error analysis (with examples and categories) and inclusion of “no-answer” questions are especially valuable.

**Limitations:** MISS-QA is specialized to scientific diagrams (mostly CS/AI papers), so results may not directly transfer to educational CS diagrams. The initial reported dataset size (1.5K Qs) is moderate; anthologized metadata suggests an expanded set (3K Qs), but this discrepancy aside, the number of examples per diagram type may still be limited. The focus is on answer accuracy; the paper does not quantitatively measure *explanation faithfulness* (it reports examples qualitatively). Also, despite CoT prompting, the evaluation judges only the final answer, not each reasoning step. In practice, the actual **reasoning chains** from models are often incomplete or oversimplified (as shown in the case study) and were not formally scored. 

**Reproducibility:** The authors have **open-sourced data and code** (GitHub: *yilunzhao/MISS-QA*)【26†L255-L263】【26†L307-L315】. The evaluation protocol is documented and automated. All 18 models and prompts can be re-used. Thus the work is highly reproducible.

# Dataset & Task

- **MISS-QA Dataset:** 1,500 (∼3,000 in final reports) question-answer pairs drawn from 465 (ultimately 983) AI-related papers【31†L54-L63】. Each example includes: (a) a **schematic diagram** (an overview figure like a system block diagram, design diagram, architecture figure), (b) a **highlighted element** in the image (with a red box), (c) the diagram’s caption, (d) relevant sections of the paper text, and (e) a question with a human-annotated answer (or marked unanswerable). Questions fall into categories like *Design Rationale*, *Implementation Details*, *Background*, *Experimental Results*, etc【26†L297-L304】. 22.5% of questions are intentionally **unanswerable** (so models must learn to say “I do not know”)【4†L125-L132】.

- **Task:** Given the diagram + context + question, a model must **locate the highlighted part** and **use the text context** to answer the question. This tests integrated diagram *interpretation* (figuring out the meaning/role of the highlighted element in the system) and *textual retrieval* (finding the answer in the paper). It is an open-ended QA task (free-form answer), scored by accuracy via an LLM judge.

# Methods & Prompting

- **Models evaluated:** 18 multimodal foundation models, split into (a) **Proprietary** (OpenAI “o4-mini”, GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, Google Gemini-2.5-Flash) and (b) **Open-source** (Qwen2-VL-72B/7B, InternVL 2.5B–38B, Pixtral-12B, Mistral-Small-3.1-24B, Phi-3.5-Vision, Phi-4-Multimodal, etc.)【7†L508-L517】. These models cover the current state of the art in vision+language.

- **Prompting:** The main experiments use a **chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt** adapted from prior work【7†L517-L525】. The system prompt instructs the model: “You are a computer science researcher. You are provided a schematic diagram… Your task is to interpret the diagram and locate question-relevant information in the paper to answer the question. If the question cannot be answered based on the paper context, clearly conclude with: ‘I do not know.’ Follow instructions and think *step by step* to answer.” The question and diagram (with caption) are then provided as user input【7†L517-L525】. This encourages the model to articulate reasoning steps. The output is the answer (with intermediate reasoning steps included, though only the final answer is scored).

- **Evaluation Metric:** **Accuracy** with partial credit: 0 (wrong), 0.5 (partial), or 1 (correct). Answers are scored automatically via an LLM-based judge. Initially, they used GPT-4 as the judge【7†L490-L498】; the official writeup suggests an *open-source LLM trained on human scores*, but either way the method compares generated answers to gold answers. A small human evaluation (two PhD students) on a subset confirms expert accuracy (~89%)【7†L499-L507】.

# Main Results

- **Overall performance:** There is a **large gap** between models and humans. Human experts got **~89% accuracy** on test questions【7†L499-L507】. The best open-source model, **Qwen2.5-VL-72B**, only achieved **61.6%** accuracy【7†L545-L552】. The best open-source graph suggests ~60% (InternVL3-38B at 60.4%)【13†L582-L590】. Proprietary models do better but still below human: GPT-4.1-mini got ~74.1%, GPT-4.1 ~77.8% (on the testmini split)【13†L578-L586】. The *o4-mini* model (OpenAI’s vision model) scored ~78.3%. Recent improvements in model families are noted: Qwen2.5-72B outperformed Qwen2-72B by ~7.4%【7†L553-L560】, and InternVL3-38B beat InternVL2.5-38B by 8.9%【7†L553-L560】. This shows rapid progress, but even new models trail human performance by ~25 points.

- **Unanswerable questions:** On the 22.5% unanswerable subset, most models **struggle to abstain**. They tend to answer anyway. For instance, Qwen2.5-VL-72B answered many “unanswerable” questions incorrectly (only 33.3% accuracy on those)【13†L582-L590】. In contrast, human experts rarely guess on these. The paper’s confusion matrices show models often answer when the ground truth is “No answer”【13†L623-L632】【13†L639-L649】. The authors note an “overconfidence” failure mode: even top models continue to give spurious answers instead of responding “I don’t know”【13†L604-L612】.

- **Example performance (case study):** In one example diagram (AdaBridge DNN system, Figure 6) the question asks about the importance of the “iterative parameter updating” module. The model (Qwen2-VL-72B) output a generic answer focusing only on a “Task module” role and did not mention the *key idea* of “dynamic switching and incremental updating to optimize representation capacity”【16†L733-L742】. The error analysis notes that the model “misses the core technical importance” and fails to discuss how incremental updating addresses evolving tasks【16†L743-L751】. This illustrates how answers can superficially sound plausible but omit the true reasoning.

# Error Analysis

From 100 sampled errors (50 from Qwen2.5-72B, 50 from InternVL2.5-38B), the authors identify five common flaws【16†L755-L764】【16†L771-L779】:

- **Diagram interpretation failure:** The model cannot decode the diagram’s structure or identify the highlighted element. It might ignore the highlight or misunderstand its role.
- **Context retrieval failure:** The model misses the relevant text sections of the paper, retrieving unrelated information or answering partially.
- **Reasoning error:** Even after finding the right info, the model reasons incorrectly. For example, it might misapply an algorithmic concept or give an oversimplified justification.
- **Overconfident on unanswerable:** When no answer exists in the context, the model often still fabricates an answer (fails to say “I don’t know”), due to its tendency to respond rather than admit uncertainty.
- **Over-reliance on visuals:** Some models answer by looking mostly at the image and using “parametric” knowledge about common diagram elements, rather than integrating the text context. They may ignore crucial textual cues.

These failure modes highlight that the models’ internal reasoning chains are often **unfaithful or incomplete**: they may mention some reasoning steps but miss critical connections (as in Fig.6). 

# Strengths and Weaknesses

- **Strengths:** The study is thorough and timely. MISS-QA fills a gap in multimodal QA by focusing on **schematic diagrams** rather than just charts or raw images【4†L111-L119】. The dataset is built from real research papers, with expert annotations ensuring quality. Evaluating a wide range of models (closed and open) provides a clear survey of current capabilities. The CoT prompt and LLM-based scoring are sensible choices. The paper’s insights (e.g. performance on unanswerable questions, error categories) are valuable for guiding future model improvements.

- **Weaknesses:** The domain (scientific research diagrams) is specialized; it’s unclear how well these findings generalize to, say, textbook diagrams or simpler block diagrams in undergraduate CS materials. The original dataset size (1.5K questions) is modest (though the final version reportedly doubled this). Also, while the prompt elicited step-by-step answers, the evaluation focuses only on the final answer accuracy. There is no quantitative metric for *explanation faithfulness* (we only see qualitative examples). In practice, the “step by step” chains were often glossed over by models, and the paper does not deeply analyze the quality of those chains.

- **Reproducibility:** Very good. Data and code are released (GitHub and HuggingFace) as cited on the paper and README【26†L255-L263】【26†L307-L315】. The evaluation scripts are provided, and all model details (versions, sizes) are documented. Any researcher can reproduce the benchmark results given access to the models.

# Relevance to Research Question

Our research question is: *“Can multimodal models explain the step-by-step reasoning behind solutions to image-based computing problems?”* 

- **(a) Schematic diagrams:** This paper is directly on *diagram understanding*. The task requires models to parse and interpret schematic figures (system block diagrams, architectures) – exactly an image-based representation of computing concepts. It shows that current models struggle with this. 

- **(b) Step-by-step explanation (CoT):** The study *used* a chain-of-thought prompt, instructing models to “think step by step”【7†L517-L525】. However, model responses often lacked depth. The error analysis shows they missed key reasoning steps (e.g. in Fig.6) and sometimes produced superficial justifications. Thus, even when prompted for CoT, the models’ *actual* reasoning traces are incomplete. This suggests we must be skeptical about trusting their purported step-by-step answers.

- **(c) Faithfulness of explanations:** The results highlight issues of faithfulness. Models can give seemingly plausible answers that are actually incorrect or incomplete. The AdaBridge example【16†L743-L751】 and the discussion of “oversimplification” show that model explanations often omit critical reasoning. Likewise, models frequently hallucinate answers. Therefore, their explanations are not reliably faithful to the true reasoning from the image and text.

- **(d) Spatial/structural reasoning:** Schematic diagrams are highly structural. The paper finds models fail to decode that structure (error type 1)【16†L755-L764】. This echoes issues seen in other work (like FlowVQA) with spatial logic. It confirms that structural/spatial reasoning is a weak point. The models often cannot “follow the diagram” properly.

- **(e) Implications for CS assessment:** Although the paper focuses on research schematics, the implications carry over to CS education. Many CS exam problems use diagrams (flowcharts, state machines, network diagrams). If models can’t reliably explain these, students using such tools might get wrong or shallow answers. In fact, related work (Parsons problems) shows GPT-4V easily solves image-based CS problems (96.7% accuracy)【38†L112-L121】, meaning students could use these tools to bypass learning. This paper suggests that *complex* diagram questions (beyond simple coding puzzles) remain a challenge. For CS assessment, it underscores that while generative AI is powerful, multimodal reasoning in structured diagrams is not yet foolproof, and careful design of questions and evaluation of model explanations is needed.

# Takeaway

**Zhao *et al.* (ACL 2025)** introduce MISS-QA, a novel benchmark of schematic-diagram question answering, and show that even the best multimodal models (e.g. GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen2.5) achieve well under **70% accuracy** – far below expert humans (~89%)【7†L545-L552】【13†L584-L591】. Models often misinterpret the diagram or retrieve wrong context, and give confabulatory answers on impossible questions【13†L604-L612】【16†L755-L764】. Crucially, their chain-of-thought justifications can omit key steps (as in the AdaBridge example【16†L733-L742】), indicating poor faithfulness. In short, current VLMs can attempt step-by-step reasoning on image-based CS problems, but they frequently fail to reason correctly; their explanations should be viewed with caution.
